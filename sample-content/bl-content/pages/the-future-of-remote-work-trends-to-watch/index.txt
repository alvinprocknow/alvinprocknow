<p>Five years on from the forced experiment of 2020, remote and hybrid work has settled into something more deliberate. Companies aren't scrambling to figure out whether it works anymore &mdash; most have already decided, one way or another &mdash; and the conversation has shifted to how to do it well instead of whether to do it at all.</p>

<p>We pulled survey data from a handful of industry reports and combined it with our own conversations with hiring managers across a few different sectors. Three trends came up again and again:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Async-first documentation is replacing status meetings.</strong> Teams are investing more in writing things down once than repeating them in four timezone-staggered calls.</li>
  <li><strong>"Hub and spoke" offices are replacing the all-remote or all-in-office binary.</strong> Small regional hubs for occasional in-person collaboration, with most work happening from home.</li>
  <li><strong>Compensation is slowly decoupling from location.</strong> Still uneven across industries, but the gap between "local" and "remote" pay bands has narrowed noticeably.</li>
</ol>

<p>The numbers back this up. Here's a snapshot of how things have shifted over the past three years, based on aggregated survey responses from mid-size tech companies:</p>

<p>None of this suggests remote work is going away &mdash; it suggests it's maturing. The novelty phase, where everyone was improvising, is over. What's left is a set of practices that are genuinely being refined rather than reinvented every quarter.</p>

<blockquote>
  <p>"We stopped asking 'should people come into the office' and started asking 'what is the office actually for.' That second question turned out to be much more useful."</p>
</blockquote>
